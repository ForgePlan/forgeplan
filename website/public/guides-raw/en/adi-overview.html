ADI · OVERVIEW

ADI: three-version analysis as a daily practice

A good investigator does not lock onto the first version. They write down several, describe a testable consequence for each one - and only then collect measurements. ADI (the FPF ADI cycle: Abduction → Deduction → Induction) carries this move into engineering practice: three versions of the cause, one of which has to refute you.

An analogy with differential diagnosis

Differential diagnosis serves here as a teaching analogy: consider competing explanations of symptoms rather than just one. This helps illustrate the risk of anchoring on the first explanation. It is not a claim about ADI's medical origin, how often diagnoses change, or a mandatory sequence of clinical work.

In our analogy, an investigator writes down several possible explanations and identifies an observation that could distinguish each one from its rivals. They then compare those explanations with data. This is a sketch of engineering reasoning, not medical guidance.

In FPF, the ADI cycle connects abduction, deduction and induction. Abduction proposes hypotheses that explain an observed fact. Deduction derives testable consequences: "if the hypothesis is true, expect X". Induction compares those consequences with data and refines the working conclusion. A match with an observation supports a hypothesis but does not prove it.

A similar structure can be used in engineering tasks. In root-cause analysis, list possible causes before choosing a fix. In threat modeling, compare several attack vectors before choosing a defence. In research, formulate competing hypotheses before an experiment. These are examples of use, not a history of ADI's spread or universal requirements of these disciplines.

What all versions share: one version is not analysis, it is a reflex. ADI is the practice that turns the reflex into analysis.

When to apply it

ADI pays off in situations where the first explanation creates a false sense of understanding. Six typical contexts.

DEBUGGING
"Why did the service start failing?"
The first version ("a memory leak") is often right, but not the only one. At the same time there can be a leak, a race condition, and degradation in a neighbouring service. Three versions keep you from stopping at the first fix.
ARCHITECTURAL CHOICE
"Why does our first option look better?"
The team proposes a solution that looks obvious at first glance. ADI forces you to write down two competing architectures and to check explicitly by which criteria they are worse - instead of just accepting "well, everyone does it".
RETROSPECTIVE
"Why did the task take three times longer?"
"We underestimated the complexity" is an answer that closes the conversation and yields no lessons. ADI unfolds it into three competing versions: we underestimated, the requirements changed, we were blocked externally. Each needs a different remedy.
METRICS DRIFT
"Conversion dropped by 12%"
The versions may be incompatible (API degradation, a change in user behaviour, a broken form on mobile). Without an explicit list of versions the team usually checks the first one, finds no "obvious culprit" and declares it "statistical noise".
DEPENDENCY FAILURE
"The external service does not respond"
The cause may be at the vendor, in the network layer, in a change of authentication, or in our own code. ADI gives an order of investigation - from most likely to least likely - instead of a chaotic "let's just restart everything".
THE FIX DID NOT WORK
"We shipped X, but nothing improved"
Versions: X is implemented incorrectly, X solves the wrong problem, the problem got worse for another reason. The third version is the most inconvenient - and it is exactly the one that gets skipped without an explicit list.

What one cycle looks like

Four steps of one pass. In FPF terms these are the three phases of the ADI cycle: Abduction (three versions), Deduction (a testable consequence for each) and Induction (comparing the consequences with the data). The time estimate below is illustrative, not a measurement: for a simple situation it is tens of minutes, for a complex architectural one - hours. What matters is that the cycle ends in a concrete action, not in an extended discussion.

01
Write down three versions of the cause
Write down three competing explanations of the observed phenomenon. The first - the one that came to mind immediately. The second - an alternative from another layer (for example, if the first was "a bug in the code", the second is "infrastructure"). The third - the "awkward" one: the explanation you want to wave away as unlikely, but which explains all the symptoms.
Example: API response time collapses. V1: connection pool leak. V2: degradation of a neighbouring service. V3: the client traffic pattern changed.
02
Describe a testable consequence for each
For every version - one question: "if this version is true, what exactly should we observe in the data". The consequence has to be testable: a specific metric, a specific range. Not "the error counter will grow", but "p95 latency will grow under load above 200 RPS".
V1: if it is a leak - pool_size in the metrics should grow monotonically. V2: if it is the neighbour - its latency should correlate with ours. V3: if it is traffic - the distribution across endpoints has changed in the logs.
03
Collect the measurements
Not "look at" the data, but deliberately check the consequence of every version. Order - from the cheapest check to the most expensive one. It matters: the measurements are taken before the decision on what to do has been made. Otherwise the action follows whichever version happens to look "obvious".
Checked: pool_size is stable. The neighbouring service's latency is flat. Request distribution: the share of heavy endpoints grew from 12% to 31%.
04
Discard the unconfirmed versions, record the conclusion
Versions whose consequences did not hold are explicitly discarded, with the reason. The version whose consequence matched the observation is accepted as the working one. If none of them held - the cycle repeats with a new set of versions. The conclusion and the discarded versions are written into the artifact.
Conclusion: the cause is a shift in the traffic profile. V1 and V2 discarded: the data did not match. Action: optimise the heavy endpoints, leave the pool alone.
The key point of step two: the consequence has to be refuting, not confirming. Not "if the version is true we will find something similar", but "if the version is true we will find exactly this - and if we do not, the version is false". The difference is subtle, and it is exactly what separates analysis from confirmation hunting.

Why exactly three

The number is not accidental, but it is not a statistical threshold either. Three versions are a working heuristic against anchoring bias: the first idea is usually based on familiarity rather than fitness; the second is the obvious alternative; the third is where the non-trivial options appear. Upstream FPF asks for a serious rival next to the selected candidate (if there is none, that has to be justified), not for a universal "three hypotheses" threshold.

1
One version
That is a reflex, not analysis. The human brain forms the first version of an explanation in a fraction of a second - from heuristics, patterns, recent experience. One version requires no effort, creates no comparison and has no self-check mechanism. Any data will be read in its favour.
2
Two versions
Better, but it creates a false choice. Psychologically two versions are perceived as a binary dilemma: "either A, or B". Teams with two versions spend their time arguing about "whose version is right" instead of looking for data. The problem may turn out to be a third one that nobody considered.
3
Three versions
They break the corridor. The third version forces you past the first two and into an explanation from another layer. This material cites no statistic of the kind "in real incidents more than 60% of the time the cause is neither the first nor the second version": the 60% figure is an illustrative estimate, not a measurement. The practical point is simpler - the third version is a way not to stop at the first two.

The number "three" is neither magic nor a measured baseline, but a working heuristic. Four and five versions make sense too, but they cost more: the effort threshold grows faster than the added value. An estimate like "the third version gives 80% protection against the first version" is illustrative and not backed by a measurement. The fourth and the fifth are for situations where the stakes are very high.

One important nuance: the third version must be from another layer, not just a variation of the first. If the first two are "a leak in the code" and "another leak in the code", the third has to be infrastructural, product or operational. Otherwise the three versions stay one corridor, only a little wider.
