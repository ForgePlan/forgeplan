TRUST CALCULUS · OVERVIEW

Trust Calculus: rating trust in a decision across three axes

A vendor's research and a measurement of your own data made by a colleague on a Friday evening may have the same "average quality". But they are different stakes. One piece of evidence confirms a decision in your context; another creates the illusion of confidence where there is none. A trust calculation is the tool that makes that difference visible.

Why evidence needs a formal rating

When a team picks between two technical options, this is usually what happens: each side brings evidence. Some bring a post from a company blog. Others a benchmark run on a different configuration. A third a link to a Reddit thread. In the meeting all of it carries roughly the same weight, because there is no scale on which to compare.

In the end the most persuasive talker wins, or whoever has more authority in the room - not whoever has the stronger evidence. Six months later, when the decision starts to crack, it turns out the key argument was marketing material rather than a real measurement.

A formal rating of evidence solves one problem: it makes the team answer three questions about every piece of evidence explicitly - before the decision is taken. Once those three answers sit next to the evidence, it becomes visible that some arguments weigh 0.8 and others weigh 0.2. The final trust calculation then reflects that, rather than the intensity of the discussion.

Without such a scale, teams systematically overrate well-presented but weak evidence and underrate rough but precise measurements. This is not about how smart the team is - it is a consequence of having no tool.

Three axes: F, G, R in plain language

This lesson's teaching model rates every piece of evidence along three independent axes. Each axis gives a score from 0 to 1. None is more important than the other two - they are different measurements of one question: "how far can this evidence be trusted in our situation?". The 0-1 scale and the band boundaries below are a teaching layout for this walkthrough; how the same axes are described in FPF itself, and what Forgeplan actually computes, is in the formula block further down.

F
Formal - how strictly the claim is worded
How precisely the evidence itself is stated. A vague conclusion "it is faster" has low rigour. A concrete claim "p95 latency 42ms at 1000 RPS on configuration X" has high rigour. One thing matters here: rigour of wording is not the same as truth. A strictly worded lie is still a lie. But rigour at least lets you check the claim.
0.0-0.3 · "faster", "better"
0.4-0.6 · partly with numbers
0.7-1.0 · metric + conditions
- · example: independent review
G
Granular - level of detail, presence of numbers
How detailed the evidence is spelled out. One number without context is low detail. A table broken down by scenario, data size and configuration is high detail. G asks: can you tell from this evidence under which conditions it holds, and how closely your case matches those conditions?
0.0-0.3 · one sentence, no detail
0.4-0.6 · data present, no breakdown
0.7-1.0 · broken down by scenario
- · example: a benchmark with a matrix
R
Reliable - how trustworthy the source is in your context
How reliable the source is - not in general, but for your task. An article from a vendor who sells the product rates low. An independent academic benchmark rates medium. A measurement made by your own team on your own infrastructure with your own data rates high. R is the most context-bound axis: the same source can score 0.9 for one task and 0.3 for another.
0.0-0.3 · vendor research
0.4-0.6 · independent publication
0.7-1.0 · your measurement on your data
- · example: a production profile

Each axis is scored separately, and they cannot compensate for one another. High detail does not make an unreliable source reliable. A reliable source does not compensate for vague wording. The overall rating of a piece of evidence is the minimum of the three numbers, not the average. That is what the next section is about.

The weakest-link rule: why the minimum, not the average

The most common question when people meet the Trust Calculus goes like this: "Why not take the average? If the source is reliable (R = 0.9), does that not compensate for the claim being vague (F = 0.2)?"

No. An average hides a critical weakness and creates a false sense of confidence. A chain of three axes is not a sum of strengths, it is a multiplication of constraints.

An everyday analogy: the roadworthiness inspection of a car. The car goes through a checklist: engine, brakes, lights, steering. If the brakes fail, the car does not drive out - not even with a perfect engine, brand-new lights and faultless steering. An average across all items could report "84% - very good", and a car with bad brakes would drive away.

Evidence works the same way. If the claim is vague and unverifiable (F = 0.2), then the detail of the data and the reputation of the source do not matter: there is no way to check whether the claim holds at all. Or if the source is vendor marketing (R = 0.2), then no matter how many numbers it prints, those numbers are not optimised for your task.

The weakest-link rule in the teaching model: evidence rating = min(F, G, R). Here we take the minimum, not the average or a weighted sum. This is not a universal FPF formula or Forgeplan's artifact F/G/R computation.

Engineers know this principle under another name - Amdahl's law in parallel computing, or the "bottleneck" of any system. The logic is the same here: a chain is exactly as reliable as its weakest link.

Where F/G/R lives inside a DDR

A DDR (Detailed Decision Record) is a decision record with six mandatory sections. The third one is called "Evidence" - and that is exactly where the Trust Calculus lives.

In the teaching example, every piece of evidence in that section is recorded not as a bare link or quotation, but as a structured object with four fields:

  • The evidence itself - what exactly: a link, a quotation, a measurement result.
  • F / G / R - three numbers from 0 to 1, one per axis.
  • The overall rating - min(F, G, R) in the teaching model.
  • Context fit level (CL) - how much this evidence applies to your situation at all.

The context fit level is a separate parameter on top of F/G/R. It answers the question: "is this evidence about us at all?". Evidence can be strict, detailed and from a reliable source - but if it concerns a load of 10 000 RPS while you run 50 RPS, its applicability is limited. The numbers in the table match Forgeplan's CL penalties (0 / 0.1 / 0.4 / 0.9 for CL3 / CL2 / CL1 / CL0), but the product subtracts them rather than using the teaching multiplier (1 − CL penalty) below.

  • CL3 · OUR CONTEXT
    Penalty: 0%
    The same scenario, the same infrastructure, the same load range. The evidence was measured under conditions as close to yours as possible.
  • CL2 · SIMILAR CONTEXT
    Penalty: 10%
    Similar load, close architecture. The evidence is relevant, but you have to adjust for the difference in conditions.
  • CL1 · DIFFERENT CONTEXT
    Penalty: 40%
    A different scale, a different technological environment. The conclusions transfer with difficulty; applicability has to be argued explicitly.
  • CL0 · OPPOSED
    Penalty: 90%
    The conditions are so different that transferring the conclusions directly would be wrong. The evidence barely works in the DDR section.

The teaching formula of this walkthrough: score = min(F, G, R) × (1 − CL penalty). In this teaching model, the minimum over the evidence in the section is labelled the decision's R_eff.

The teaching formula and the Forgeplan computation are different things. The product above is a teaching example, not a universal FPF formula or the product algorithm. Forgeplan separately computes an artifact's F/G/R on a 0–1 scale and their geometric mean (F × G × R)^(1/3); these are artifact-quality heuristics. An evidence pack's intrinsic score does not use the teaching F/G/R ratings. In the full scorer, an unexpired pack scores max(0, verdict − CL penalty − type modifier). Bases for supports / weakens / refutes are 1.0 / 0.5 / 0.0; CL0 / CL1 / CL2 / CL3 penalties are 0.9 / 0.4 / 0.1 / 0; modifiers for measurement / test / benchmark / audit are 0 / 0 / 0.1 / 0.2. If the optional valid_until has expired, the scorer returns 0.1 before this arithmetic, including for refutes. With no date, this scorer applies no expiry. Recursive decision R_eff takes the minimum of its own eligible evidence score and eligible dependency contributions. Evidence packs in draft count; superseded/deprecated packs are excluded. Dependencies also exclude draft and the note/memory kinds. A dependency contributes max(0, dependency R_eff − relation penalty): based_on/informs incur 0.1 (CL2), refines/depends_on incur 0 (CL3). Own evidence at 1.0 and an eligible based_on dependency at 0.2 produce 0.1 overall. With no eligible own evidence, an ordinary decision scores 0; a leaf evidence pack is scored on its own fields. The flat helper used for some displayed scores neither traverses dependencies nor applies the type modifier. Its result can differ from the full recursive report.

A worked example with three pieces of evidence

Let us walk through a concrete case: a team picks between two databases for storing vector embeddings - LanceDB and SQLite with the vector extension. Both options work technically; the team has collected three pieces of evidence in favour of LanceDB.

CASE · CHOOSING STORAGE FOR VECTOR SEARCH

Three pieces of evidence and their F / G / R / CL rating

The decision's final R_eff = min of all scores = 0.12. The decision is technically accepted, but the signal is weak: one of the three pieces of evidence is vendor marketing with poor context fit. That is a reason either to find a replacement for E1, or to mark it explicitly as "accepted with low trust". All three ratings are a teaching example: the numbers in the F/G/R columns were chosen to demonstrate the minimum rule, not taken from a real measurement.

# Evidence F G R min CL Rating
E1 Vendor research: "LanceDB is 5× faster on analytical queries" 0.3 0.4 0.2 0.2 CL1 −40% 0.12
E2 A publication by the Qdrant engineers with a benchmark on 1M vectors, broken down by HNSW parameters 0.8 0.8 0.6 0.6 CL2 −10% 0.54
E3 Our own team's measurement: 500K documents, our own load, comparing p95 latency and throughput 0.9 0.9 0.95 0.9 CL3 −0% 0.90
R_eff = min(0.12, 0.54, 0.90) = 0.12  ·  Weak signal - E1 (vendor research) drags it down

Notice: even one weak source drags down the R_eff of the whole decision. That is not a bug - it is the point. The weakest-link rule says: if your evidence base contains marketing material that you take seriously, that is a risk. Either remove it, or state openly that you are relying on weak evidence.

In this example the team could drop E1 from the DDR and rely on E2 and E3 alone - then R_eff = min(0.54, 0.90) = 0.54. That is already an honest rating: "we decided with moderate trust, on the basis of an independent benchmark and our own measurement".

The main point: a trust calculation is not bureaucracy on top of a discussion, it is protection against a common mistake. Teams do not lose because they pick bad technology; they lose because they trust well-presented arguments as much as real measurements. Three numbers and the minimum rule make that difference visible before the decision is taken.
