Evidence and Decay: why proofs have a shelf life

A benchmark on an old library version may not apply to a new one, even if it sits in a decision record and looks convincing. Evidence applicability depends on conditions. A review date helps you notice when it needs checking.

What counts as evidence

Evidence is a concrete source that a decision relies on. Not "based on our experience" but "here is exactly what we measured, read or heard". Four types with different weight:

  1. 01 · MEASUREMENT ON YOUR OWN DATA
    The strongest type
    A benchmark under real load, a load test in production, a profiling run on our own hardware. Bound directly to our conditions. It goes stale when the load or the infrastructure changes.
  2. 02 · A COLLEAGUE'S BENCHMARK
    Medium weight
    A measurement by another team with a similar stack and a similar load. Applicable when the context matches. It loses weight when scale, versions or architecture diverge.
  3. 03 · VENDOR DOCUMENTATION
    Depends on being current
    Official documentation, a changelog, release notes. Reliable as long as the version matches the one the project uses. After a major update it has to be re-checked.
  4. 04 · ANOTHER TEAM'S EXPERIENCE
    Weak but useful
    A case study, an article, a talk at a conference. Applicable as an argument about "direction", not as an exact forecast. It has to come together with a stricter source.

The key point: in a decision record every piece of evidence has to be tied to a type. If it only says "we ran some tests", it is unclear what exactly, on what and when. If it says "benchmark 14.11.2025, PostgreSQL 16.2, our staging, 1 000 RPS - p95 4 ms", that is evidence whose applicability you can judge a year later.

Every piece of evidence has three dimensions

To judge how applicable a piece of evidence is to your situation, three independent axes are used. Together they answer the question: "how far is this worth trusting". The 0-3 scale in the cards below is a teaching layout for this walkthrough: in FPF itself the same axes are described differently (F as an ordinal rigour of expression, G as the set of contexts the claim covers). The teaching F/G/R ratings are not inputs to a Forgeplan evidence pack's intrinsic score; the product separately computes artifact-quality F/G/R — see the block at the end of the page.

F
Rigour (Formality)
How methodologically sound the evidence is. A controlled experiment that isolates variables - high rigour. A subjective impression from a conversation - low.
F0 (impression) → F3 (controlled experiment)
G
Detail (Granularity)
How concrete the source is with respect to your task. A measurement of exactly your operation on your own hardware - high detail. A general survey of the technology without numbers - low.
G0 (survey) → G3 (exact measurement under your context)
R
Reliability (Reliability)
How much the source deserves trust. A peer-reviewed study, official documentation - high reliability. An anonymous comment in a blog - low.
R0 (anonymous) → R3 (verified source)

These three axes form this lesson's teaching model of trust (trust calculus). In this model, the resulting score is not the average of F, G and R but the minimum of the three: one weak dimension drags the whole result down. If the evidence is strict and detailed but comes from an unreliable source, its final weight is low. A detailed interactive walkthrough and the spatial model live in trust-calculus.

Shelf life - what happens to evidence over time

Evidence may have an optional valid_until field — an expiry date. If it is set and has passed, the scorer returns exactly 0.1. With no date, this scorer applies no expiry. This is not a universal minimum or necessarily a decrease: refutes can score 0 before expiry and 0.1 after.

The logic is simple: if a load test ran a year ago and the library has released three major versions since, the test may no longer be relevant. The system does not know whether anything changed; a requested report can identify a passed date. A person or explicitly configured external automation starts the evidence check, not the date itself.

Below is an invented example of manual reassessment and expiry, not an automatic gradual trust-decay function:

Day of creation - the evidence is fresh
The benchmark has just been run on the current version. All three axes F/G/R are in good shape. The weight is at maximum.
weight 1.0

The library shipped a minor update and the documentation changed twice. In this example the team manually reassessed the evidence at 0.6; approaching the date does not itself change the score.
weight 0.6
After valid_until passes - stale
During scoring, an expired date yields a fixed 0.1. A report can show the expiry, but it neither changes the stored status nor initiates review itself.
weight 0.1
After a refresh - the evidence is extended
We re-ran the measurement or found a current source. The weight is restored. The review condition in the decision record is closed and a new one is set.
weight 1.0
In this example, an expired pack at 0.1 limits trust. In general, the recursive decision score accounts for both its own eligible evidence and eligible dependencies. Expiry need not lower the result: another weak link may already impose a lower limit.
Important: Forgeplan has no gradual decay. The timeline above is a teaching picture. The real computation keeps a pack's ordinary score until the valid_until date and then returns 0.1: a step, not a curve. The valid_until field is optional - with no date, the shelf life never ends. Extending the date is not itself a new measurement: it is a manual reassessment, and a person has to make it.

Review triggers when the shelf life ends

Dates, events and metrics are three kinds of review conditions a team can record. The built-in report can identify expired dates, but it does not reopen a decision or recheck evidence. A person or explicitly configured external automation must initiate review. The team or an external monitor checks metrics and events.

Trigger type What happens Recommended action
DATE
Expiry date		 The valid_until field of an evidence pack expires. The tool finds such records and shows them as overdue; the status of the artifact does not change by itself. Re-run the measurement or find a current source. If it is still valid - extend the date. If it is stale - revisit the decision.
EVENT
Event in the project		 A major dependency update, an architecture change, a tenfold growth in load. Written explicitly into the "conditions for revisiting" field. There is no automatic check for such a condition. Open the record and check whether the context changed so much that the decision has to be reconsidered.
METRIC
Metric threshold		 DAU passed 10 000, latency grew past 200 ms, infrastructure cost doubled. Checked by an external monitor. Revisit the decision as part of an incident review or a scheduled metric audit. The trigger is recorded as a condition up front.

Setting up an automatic notification depends on your working environment. The simplest way: add a repeating event to your calendar with a link to the decision record. If your log lives next to the code, you can put a CI script in place that checks the valid_until fields and warns during the build. What matters is not the particular implementation but that the notification exists and fires on its own - without relying on somebody's memory. There is no ready-made built-in monitor: you set up that script or external watcher yourself.

A typical mistake is to write the review condition as "if the situation changes". That is not a condition, it is an intention. A condition is "if p95 latency rises above 200 ms" or "if a major version of the library ships". A concrete condition can be checked manually or assigned an external monitor; recording it does not start automation.

What Forgeplan actually stores. Forgeplan separately computes artifact-quality F/G/R on a 0–1 scale and their geometric mean (F × G × R)^(1/3). The teaching F/G/R ratings are not inputs to an evidence pack's intrinsic score (kind: evidence). In the full scorer, an unexpired pack scores max(0, verdict − CL penalty − type modifier). Bases for supports/weakens/refutes are 1.0 / 0.5 / 0.0; CL0 / CL1 / CL2 / CL3 penalties are 0.9 / 0.4 / 0.1 / 0; modifiers for measurement/test/benchmark/audit are 0 / 0 / 0.1 / 0.2. An expired optional valid_until takes precedence over the arithmetic: the score is 0.1, even for refutes. With no date, this scorer applies no expiry. Recursive decision R_eff takes the minimum of its own eligible evidence score and eligible dependency contributions. Evidence packs in draft count; superseded/deprecated packs are excluded. Dependencies also exclude draft and the note/memory kinds. A dependency contributes max(0, dependency R_eff − relation penalty): based_on/informs incur 0.1 (CL2), refines/depends_on incur 0 (CL3). Own evidence at 1.0 and an eligible based_on dependency at 0.2 produce 0.1 overall. With no eligible own evidence, an ordinary decision scores 0; a leaf evidence pack is scored on its own fields. The flat helper used for some displayed scores neither traverses dependencies nor applies the type modifier. Its result can differ from the full recursive report. If verdict or congruence_level is not recognised, the parser forces CL0 (penalty 0.9): no silent maximum trust is granted.

The main point: a missing date does not stop a source from being evidence. An optional valid_until helps set a time to check applicability. The tool shows an expired date in a report; this does not change the stored status. A person or explicitly configured external automation is needed to check the source and revisit the decision if necessary.
