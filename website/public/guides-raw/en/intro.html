DECISION LOG · FOUNDATION

What a decision log is and why a team needs one

Every six months, in every team, the same question comes up: «why did we do it this way». Sometimes someone remembers. More often, nobody does. That recurring pain grew into a separate engineering practice: decision logging. The specific formats (ADR, DDR), the six mandatory fields of a record, why «average» misleads when you weigh evidence, and why every record needs a revisit condition.

What a decision log is

A decision log is an engineering practice of recording every architectural and product choice in one structured format. The goal is that a year from now anyone can open a record and understand: what was decided, why exactly that way, and under what conditions the question needs to be revisited.

An analogy from software: git stores the history of changes to code. Who changed what, and when; any version is easy to return to. A decision log does the same thing for decisions: who chose one of the options, when, and why.

An analogy for anyone who does not work with git: picture the logbook of a ship's captain. Every serious decision goes in there - why the course was changed, why the speed was adjusted, what the conditions were at the time, and under what circumstances you would return to the original plan. Six months later the captain or their successor can open the log and see the whole picture.

The practice has established format names. ADR was worked out by engineering teams and became a shared language. DDR and Journal Entry in this series are our own working conventions, not a ratified external standard:

  • ADR (Architecture Decision Record) - a short record of an architectural decision. Minimum: context, decision, consequences.
  • DDR (Detailed Decision Record) - an extended form where evidence and revisit conditions are recorded explicitly. This is our series' working convention; we dig into it further below.
  • Journal Entry - a simple format for teams whose architecture changes often: date, event and reason only.

A familiar scenario

Monday, work chat. A new developer asks: «Why do we use library X here instead of Y? I see X in the code, but the project documentation says almost nothing about it».

Silence in the chat for ten minutes. Someone finally answers: «We discussed this six months ago. There was a long argument, we picked X. I don't remember the reasons - something about performance, I think. Or the license. Alex will know for sure, but he's on holiday now».

Familiar? This is the standard scenario that repeats in most teams. Not because the team is bad, but because there is no place where decisions are required to land. The chat scrolls away. The wiki is forgotten. A file called «meeting-notes-2025-03-12.docx» sits in a shared folder and nobody opens it, because nobody knows what is in it.

A decision log is an attempt to give decisions a concrete place. Not «one more subscription tool», but a file next to the code, in a clear structure, with mandatory fields. When a new developer asks «why X» six months later, the answer is one link to a file - not «I think we discussed it».

Is this for you?

Four typical readers for whom decision logging pays off visibly. You may recognise one of them - yourself, or a colleague.

PRODUCT MANAGER / TEAM LEAD
The team reopens old arguments every few months

Every six months somebody asks «why did we do it this way», and nobody remembers. The team sits down, spends a couple of days rethinking it, and reaches the same conclusion. Six months later - again.

It hurts most after a key person leaves.
AI ENGINEER · SHIPS CODE WITH AN AGENT

A new session with Claude Code, Cursor or another assistant may lack the reasons for choosing library X over Y. Without them, the agent may propose a rejected option. Give it access to the record and instructions to read it, or explicitly load it into the session.

AUTOMATION ENGINEER

«Check the data for duplicates first, then run the processing, then notify the team». The rules exist, but they are written down nowhere as a contract. When the person who remembered them leaves - the automation breaks. When a new task arrives - it gets rewritten from scratch.

Process rules are decisions too, and they need a place.
DEVELOPER ON A TEAM OF 5+ PEOPLE
Context leaks away between shifts and weeks

You picked up a ticket a colleague started two weeks ago. The code is half-written, the comments hint at things, but why this particular structure was chosen is unclear. You go to the author. The author does not remember. You reconstruct the logic through messages and guesswork.

The bigger the team, the faster context washes out.

What «a decision» means in this approach

When I say «decision», I mean a concrete structure of six mandatory fields. If even one field is missing, it is not a decision - it is a record with a hole in it. These six fields are the working convention of the DDR format (Detailed Decision Record) that we use in this series of lessons, not a universally accepted external standard.

  1. 01 · CONTEXT
    The situation the decision was taken in
    What was known. Which constraints applied. Which requirements existed. Without this, a year later nobody can tell how far the decision still applies.
  2. 02 · OPTIONS
    What was considered
    At least three. Not «X or Y», but three full options with brief pros and cons. Otherwise the choice is almost always a false one.
  3. 03 · EVIDENCE
    What the choice rested on
    Concrete references: a measurement on our own data, documentation, a colleague's experience. Every piece of evidence carries a reliability assessment relative to our context.
  4. 04 · DECISION
    One option, one line
    Exactly what was chosen. No «we settled on», no «we decided to try» - a concrete statement you can check against.
  5. 05 · REJECTED
    Why the others did not work
    Every rejected alternative with a concrete reason. Not «it did not work», but «it did not work, because X». A year later that reason is either still valid or out of date.
  6. 06 · REVISIT CONDITION
    When to come back to this question
    The most important and most often skipped section. On which event, metric or date the decision needs another look. A person or explicitly configured external automation performs the check; the record does not initiate it itself.
The sixth section is the main difference between a living decision and a dead monument. Without it a record becomes «we did write something down, leave it alone» a year later, even when the context has completely changed. People sometimes call this section an «expiry condition» or a «trigger for revisit» - the idea is the same.

Why artifacts instead of chats or wikis

The main objection I hear: «We already write everything in Slack, Notion or Confluence. Why yet another format?» The answer: it is not about the amount, it is about the format. Free text does not force you to record what turns out to be needed a year later.

Before and after: one day in a team's life

To stay out of pure theory - a comparison of two teams working on the same typical task: «choose a payment provider for a new European market».

WITHOUT A DECISION LOG
  • Monday: a chat discussion, four messages arguing for different options.
  • Wednesday: a one-hour meeting, provider A is picked.
  • Friday: a developer starts the integration.
  • Four months later: a lawyer raises a tax reporting question. Nobody remembers whether it was considered during the choice.
  • The team sits down for two weeks and compares providers all over again.
  • Result: two weeks of lost time, and the risk of repeating it in six months.
WITH A DECISION LOG
  • Monday: a chat discussion - the same four messages.
  • Tuesday: 30 minutes filling in a decision record against the six-field template.
  • Wednesday: a half-hour meeting (shorter, because the options are already written down).
  • Friday: a developer starts the integration, reads the record first.
  • Four months later: the lawyer raises the question. Open the record - tax reporting was considered separately. If not, open a review for an hour.
  • Result: half an hour now, two hours in four months. Instead of two weeks.

Use-cases across domains

Although the examples above were about software development, decision logging applies anywhere a team makes decisions that will later be hard to reconstruct. Six concrete domains.

PRODUCT
Choosing an integration vendor

A payment provider, an analytics tool, an SMS vendor. A year later - the market has moved, the technology has changed. A record with revisit conditions lets you come back on a trigger instead of on somebody's sudden recollection.

INFRASTRUCTURE
Choosing a database architecture

Which database, which message broker, which caching system. Decisions with a three-to-five-year horizon. Without a record, a year later it hurts most for new team members who walk around asking «why is it done this way».

SECURITY
Threat model and risk zones

Which threats are accepted, which are not. Without a record, a year later an audit asks «why did you not protect this vector» and nobody remembers that it was consciously left unprotected.

MACHINE LEARNING
Choosing a model and its parameters

Which base model, which dataset, which decision thresholds. After six months the model is stale and the metrics have drifted - a review with a record takes a day, without a record it takes two weeks of «let us first reconstruct how we ever chose this».

EMBEDDED SYSTEMS
Choosing a protocol and a microcontroller

Decisions that stay in the hardware for five to ten years. Three years later a new developer tries to understand why there is a slow protocol here instead of a fast one. Without a record nobody remembers that only the slow one was cheap.

B2B SALES
Decisions about major accounts

We agreed to a 15% discount because the customer had strong negotiating leverage. Eight months later the customer asks for more. The record shows that the earlier decision was a compromise, not the norm - and the team can decline confidently.

Why this matters right now, in the age of AI

One specific turn made me invest in this: AI changes how we make decisions, and not for the better by default.

When a developer writes code with an agent (Claude Code, Cursor, Codex), a new session may lack the history behind choosing library X. If the reasons for choosing X and rejecting Y are not in its context, the agent may propose Y again. This depends on the available context and session settings; it does not necessarily happen at every start.

A smarter model alone does not replace project context. The model needs something to read at the start: give it access to the records and instructions to read the relevant files, or explicitly load them into the session. A file next to the code does not load itself into context or guarantee that the agent reads or correctly applies it.

The same logic applies to automation: give the agent the current process rules and instruct it to read them. «Check duplicates first, then send the notification, do not send after 22:00» - these are process rules written as decisions. Access makes the record available to consult, but correct execution of the process still needs to be checked.

The short version: decision records can provide context for AI agents. Keep them accessible and explicitly say what to read, or load the relevant records into the session. This helps communicate conventions but does not guarantee that they will be followed.
