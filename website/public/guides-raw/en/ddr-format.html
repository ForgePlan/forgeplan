DECISION LOG · FORMAT

What a good DDR looks like - a template with an example

A good DDR should be readable in two minutes. If you open a record and after two minutes you still do not know what was chosen, why, and under what conditions it should be revisited - the record was written wrong. We break the format into parts and show what it looks like on an illustrative example.

What a DDR is

DDR - Detailed Decision Record - is an extended form of decision record. The classic ADR (Architecture Decision Record) has existed for more than ten years and is essentially four fields: status, context, decision, consequences. For many cases that is enough.

An important caveat: the six fields we walk through below are our own working convention for this series of lessons, not a widely accepted external standard. Real ADR templates in projects often contain more sections.

DDR appeared once it became clear that two things are easy to skip in a classic ADR, and both make a record useless a year later. The first is evidence: what exactly the choice rested on. The second is the revisit condition: on which event you need to come back to the question. Without evidence the record looks like an opinion. Without a revisit condition it turns into a monument - nobody touches it any more, even when the context has long since changed.

How the formats relate:

  • ADR - context, decision, consequences. Fast, minimal. Good for small technical choices with a short horizon.
  • DDR - everything from the ADR plus explicit evidence and revisit conditions. A little more to write, but the record stays alive after one year and three.

The difference is not in length - it is in content. A 250-word DDR is better than a 250-word ADR whenever it carries evidence and a revisit condition.

Six mandatory fields

If even one field is missing - it is not a DDR, it is a stub. It is exactly the mandatory nature of the fields that separates a living record from free text.

  1. 01 · CONTEXT
    The situation the decision was taken in
    What was known, which constraints applied, what the scale was. Without this, a year later nobody can tell whether the decision still applies or the context has changed completely.
  2. 02 · OPTIONS
    What was considered
    At least three options, each with a brief plus and minus. If there are fewer than two, there was no choice - and the record should say so explicitly.
  3. 03 · EVIDENCE
    What the choice rested on
    Concrete sources: a measurement on your own data, the vendor's documentation, a colleague's experience from another team. Each piece of evidence carries a short note on how far it applies to your context.
  4. 04 · DECISION
    One option, one line
    The exact wording of the chosen option. No «we settled on», no «we decided to try». This line lets a year later check unambiguously whether the decision is still being followed.
  5. 05 · REJECTED
    Why the other options did not work
    Every rejected option with a concrete reason. Not «it does not work», but «it does not work, because X». A year later that reason is either still valid or out of date - and that in itself is a signal to revisit.
  6. 06 · REVISIT CONDITIONS
    When to come back to this question
    The most important and most often skipped section. A concrete event, metric or date at which the record needs to be opened again. Without this field the record is dead from day one.
The sixth field is the main difference between a DDR and a note. A note describes the past. A revisit condition says when a person or explicitly configured external automation needs to check the decision.

Full example

Below is the shape of a DDR file for a typical decision: choosing an authorization mechanism for a note-taking app used by a small team. Three options: JWT tokens, server-side sessions, OAuth through a third-party provider. The fields are filled in the way they would look in practice. The example itself is illustrative: the dates, numbers and team names inside it show the shape of a record, not the results of real measurements. 

# DDR-007: Authorization mechanism - JWT vs sessions vs OAuth

Date:       2025-11-14
Status:     active
Author:      Mikhail D.
Revisit if:  DAU exceeds 5,000, or an SSO requirement appears

## Context

A personal notes app - 4 developers, one Node.js backend,
planned DAU of 200-800 people. The only sign-in method required is
email + password. No SSO requirement, no corporate customers.
Infrastructure: a single server with no horizontal scaling.

## Options

A. JWT tokens
   + No server-side state storage required.
   − Token revocation is hard: needs a blacklist, or a short TTL + refresh.

B. Server-side sessions (httpOnly cookie + session store)
   + Simple revocation: delete the entry from the store.
   + Mature libraries, minimal client-side logic.
   − Requires permanent server-side storage (a Redis instance or a DB table).

C. OAuth through a third-party provider (Google, GitHub)
   + No password logic of our own.
   − Dependency on an external service; needs a domain and HTTPS from day one.
   − Complicates onboarding: users without a Google/GitHub account.

## Evidence

1. An in-team benchmark: a server-side session with a PostgreSQL store -
   p95 session check of 4 ms on our own hardware (measurement 14.11.2025,
   1,000 concurrent requests). Enough for our load.

2. express-session documentation: pg-simple store support,
   production-ready, last updated November 2025.

3. Experience of Arkady's team (similar stack, similar DAU): they moved
   from JWT to sessions, because the JWT blacklist added more complexity
   than the missing state saved. Subjectively applicable to our situation.

## Decision

We use server-side sessions: express-session + pg-simple store in the same
PostgreSQL database the project already has.

## Rejected options

A (JWT): rejected, because without horizontal scaling the advantage of JWT
over sessions disappears, while token revocation is more complex.

C (OAuth): rejected, because it adds a dependency on an external service
and complicates onboarding for our target audience.

## Revisit conditions

1. DAU exceeds 5,000 - check whether pg-simple store has become a bottleneck.
2. An SSO requirement appears from a corporate customer - move to OAuth/SAML.
3. A move to horizontal scaling - revisit the store (Redis).

Notice a few things. First: the evidence is concrete. Not «from our experience», but «measurement 14.11.2025, 1,000 requests, 4 ms»: in the example, «from our experience» is replaced by a date, a volume and a number. Second: in the rejected options every reason refers to our own context specifically - not «JWT is complex», but «the advantage of JWT disappears without horizontal scaling». Third: the revisit conditions are tied to concrete triggers rather than to an abstract «when necessary».

Length and readability

A good DDR fits in 200-400 words of body text. That is one screen at a standard font size. If a record is longer - it is most likely one of two things: either several unrelated decisions are being crammed into it (split them into separate records), or the explanation is written where a reference belongs.

Three guidelines for field length:

CONTEXT
3-5 sentences
OPTIONS
2-3 lines per option
EVIDENCE
1-2 sentences per source
DECISION
1 line
REJECTED
1-2 lines per option
CONDITIONS
2-4 items

The most common mistake is a huge context (5-10 paragraphs) in which everything anyone knew gets written down, plus quotes from documentation. That is not context, that is the minutes of a meeting. Context is the minimum needed for a year later to understand the constraints under which the decision was taken.

The second mistake is a decision spread over three paragraphs. The «Decision» field is one statement. The argumentation belongs in the other fields. If you want to explain the decision - look at the «Evidence» and «Rejected» fields.

The test: give the record to somebody who was not in the meeting. If in two minutes of reading they can say what was chosen and why - the length is right. If they start scrolling to find the decision - the record is overloaded.

The point: a DDR is not longer than an ADR - it is more precise. The two sections the format adds - evidence and revisit conditions - turn a record from «we decided so» into «we decided so, here is why, and here is when to check whether it has gone stale». That is what separates a living log from an archive.
