FPF: a map of consistency

FPF—the First Principles Framework—is a framework for explicit reasoning. Read it as a map of what needs to stay consistent across a team, AI agents, and your own understanding six months from now.

FPF is authored by Anatoly Levenchuk (github.com/ailev/FPF). This is a reader-oriented overview, not a restatement of the specification. It connects seven selected FPF concepts. It is neither an exhaustive definition of the kernel nor a product algorithm.

Seven selected FPF concepts

These seven connected concepts are a selection, not a sequence of steps or a complete FPF checklist. They are questions worth keeping consistent across participants, sessions, and versions of a decision.

Overview map · 7 selected FPF concepts

The nodes are not a hierarchy. They are constraints that apply together. If the vocabulary differs between participants, their reasoning can concern different objects. The reasoning cycle at the center is not a starting point but a mechanism linking a hypothesis to its expression, evidence, and practice.

Holon asks about scale: are we discussing a subsystem or the whole system? Context identifies where and for whom a term has meaning. Distinctions prevent category confusion: a role, method, plan, and actual work occupy different conceptual slots. Substituting one for another can break coordination.

Trust F-G-R in current upstream FPF distinguishes F, ordinal rigor of expression (anchors F0–F9); G, the claim’s scope (ClaimScope); and R, claim-specific warrant. G does not mean granularity here, and a numerical interpretation of R needs a justified model. This is not a universal min(F,G,R) formula. Validity sets a reason to reassess evidence: a date does not erase an observed fact, and an event needs a person or monitor to observe it. Open / closed world distinguishes “unknown” from “absent.” The reasoning cycle connects hypotheses, testable consequences, and observations. These upstream definitions were checked against revision 34a1ae7, B.3 and C.2. Distinguish that edition from older editions and local numerical rubrics.

Common confusions

Three kinds of misunderstanding deserve separate names. Each needs a different response. A generic request to “communicate better” or “clarify the requirements” can miss the underlying mismatch. First identify what is inconsistent.

Confusion 01

Role ≠ method ≠ result

“Testing isn’t working” can mean several things. Was nobody assigned the role? Is the method undefined? Is a result missing? These are different problems with different remedies. FPF asks you to name exactly what is inconsistent before discussing a solution.

Confusion 02

A word needs context

In one domain, “user” means a table record with access permissions. In another, it means a person with a session cookie. Without explicit domain boundaries, handing work between teams can introduce bugs that look like miscommunication but are really different referents behind the same word.

Confusion 03

Plan ≠ completed work

“The release is ready”—does a plan exist, or is the work complete? FPF asks you to state what exists now: an intention, a description, an artifact, or an observed fact. Confusing a plan with the world can produce false readiness claims.

When is the extra explicitness useful?

Definitions and records take effort. Judge their value for the task: if context is stable and feedback is cheap, a short record may be enough. When context crosses people or sessions and mistakes are expensive, a more explicit reasoning record may be worth the effort.

More useful Less need for detail
Work is distributed across people and AI agents; context does not transfer automatically One person retains the task context
Real-world checks are slow and expensive; trial and error is costly Feedback is quick and cheap; an error can be corrected in a minute
Different audiences need different views of the same object One reader and one perspective
New tasks strain the vocabulary; the same words mean different things to participants The vocabulary is stable and shared
Reasoning must be reusable and transferable to another agent A one-off answer for the immediate situation is enough
