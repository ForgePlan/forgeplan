01 · BMAD - four phases from idea to handover

Concept 01 · the architect's analogy

An architect does not start with blueprints

Picture an architect designing a residential building. They do not start with blueprints. First - a brief with the client: who will live there, what budget, what neighbours. Then the concept: how many floors, where the kitchen, which load-bearing structure. Then the choice of materials and systems - reinforced concrete or glued timber, central heating or a heat pump. And only then the drawings for the workers.

Skip any phase and you get a house without a foundation or a kitchen without a window. BMAD is exactly such a sequence, only for product features.

The phase names below are our own working layout for this breakdown, not the names used in BMAD itself. In BMAD (v6.2.0) the phases are its own: Analysis (optional) → Planning → Solutioning → Implementation.

  • Brainstorming · widening the space of decisions. One line from Slack turns into 3-5 ways this could be done. ADI fits here: three hypotheses, each testable. The goal is not to choose, but to see.
  • Modeling · structuring. The chosen direction breaks down into user stories (who → wants what → why), a data model (which entities appear), a state machine. Edge cases surface here.
  • Architecting · choosing a solution. From the options that survived modeling, you honestly take one. Trade-offs, risks, decisions.
  • Delivery · artifacts. The final PRD, test plan, migration plan, rollout checklist. What you can hand to the team and go get coffee.

Live example · Vault · «Tags + Saved Searches»

The feature «Tags + Saved Searches» currently looks like one task. At the Brainstorming phase it splits into three options: (1) tags as free-form strings in a JSON field on the note, (2) tags as a separate table with a many-to-many relation, (3) tags as a hierarchy with parents.

At Modeling the edge cases appear: «what if the tag already exists?», «can a note have 100 tags?», «how do you delete a tag nobody uses any more?». At Architecting you explicitly pick option 2 (a separate table) and record the justification. At Delivery - a PRD with 13 sections, the migration, the tests.

BMAD is not about bureaucracy

The four questions - what? how do we break it down? how do we implement it? how do we hand it over? - get asked in any case. In a bad process they are asked in the code and answered by an incident in production. In a good one they are asked in a document a day before the work starts.

02 · 13-step PRD - a format that will not let edge cases slip

Concept 02 · the pre-flight checklist analogy

Thirteen mandatory sections, without which the project «forgets» an edge case

So BMAD gave four phases. At the end of the Delivery phase there is a PRD (Product Requirements Document). But a PRD is not free text. It is a rigidly structured document of 13 mandatory sections.

One caveat: this list of 13 sections is our own working template for this series of lessons, not a requirement of BMAD itself. Upstream, the composition of a PRD is defined separately and is not obliged to consist of 13 items. The three highlighted sections below are our choice for this breakdown too, not a BMAD gate.

The analogy is a pilot's pre-flight checklist. Forty items, none of them optional, because a forgotten one means a crash. The list is not about bureaucracy, but about the fact that a pilot physically cannot hold 40 things in their head, and reading a checklist is cheaper than a crash. A PRD works the same way: 13 items, without which the project «forgets» an edge case and fails in production.

3 sections are highlighted: they get skipped on reflex

We single out three of these thirteen as critical (this is this breakdown's choice, not a BMAD gate) - because engineers skip them on reflex:

  • 04 · Non-Goals · an explicit list of what we will not do. For Tags that is «no tag hierarchy in the MVP», «no colours», «no sync between devices». Without it the scope creeps.
  • 12 · Open Questions · what is not clear right now. If you do not write it down, the brain makes assumptions silently, and a week later a hypothesis, not a decision, ends up in the code.
  • 10 · Risks · what could break. Without explicit risks the first incident in production catches everyone by surprise.
Non-Goals for the Tags feature · example ## Non-Goals - We do NOT support a tag hierarchy (parent / child) in the MVP - We do NOT add colours or icons for tags - We do NOT sync tags between devices - We do NOT integrate with external tagging systems (Notion, Obsidian)

Four lines, and they are exactly what saves you from a scope monster.

The agent writes the PRD · you do the manual steps

The BMAD PM agent (John) generates the first version. But the three quality checkpoints - Information Density (step 3), Measurability (step 5), Implementation Leakage (step 7) - are a manual pass. Without it the PRD stays a bag of words. Compare: «the system should be convenient» (empty) against POST /notes/:id/tags returns 200 in < 50ms on a base of 1000 notes (measurable). A team of five can work with the second. With the first - no.

03 · Adversarial Review - how to check a PRD with someone else's eyes

Concept 03 · the red team analogy

Authors do not see their own typos - that is how perception works, not laziness

You wrote a 13-step PRD and passed the three manual gates. How do you know it has no hidden contradictions, no missed edge cases and no wishful thinking? The answer is there is none, if you check it yourself. Authors do not see their own typos - that is a physiological property of perception, not a weakness of will.

The solution is Adversarial Review. You open a new empty agent session. You give it the role of a critic of this PRD with an explicit instruction: find at least 3 problems, do not praise, look for contradictions and gaps.

Prompt for Adversarial Review # Run an Adversarial Review of this PRD. Rules: 1. You MUST find at least 3 problems. 0 problems = a bad review. 2. For each problem state: section and line, severity (Critical / Warning / Pass), description, proposed fix. 3. Do NOT praise the document. Look for: implementation leakage, untestable requirements, filler, error scenarios, internal contradictions. 4. Write the report to docs/specs/REVIEW.md.

The «at least 3» threshold is this lesson's setting. BMAD's own adversarial-review prompt asks for no fewer than ten findings, so three is a more modest requirement, not a rule of the method.

The analogy is a red team in security. You never test your own defence from the inside - you bring in people whose job is to break it. They find holes the builders of the defence never saw, because they thought in terms of «how is this supposed to work» rather than «how can this be broken».

The expected result in this scheme is 5-7 findings; that is an illustrative scenario, not a measured rate of findings. Your task is not to accept everything, but to classify: REAL problem / FALSE POSITIVE (the agent missed that an AC covers it) / NITPICK (style, does not block). The model will find problems even where there are none - the filtering is manual.

New session · hard rule

If you run the review in the same context in which the PRD was written, the model defends its previous answer. This is not bad intent - it is how attention works: fresh text is «more convincing» than an abstract rule «look for problems». A clean session reads the PRD as a third-party document and reads it without bias.

The cycle on one feature · Tags + Saved Searches

How all three concepts fold into one pass - from a line in Slack to a PRD that has passed outside review.

01 · Brainstorming

Widening the space of decisions

«We need tags on notes» from Slack splits into three options: (1) free-form strings in JSON, (2) a separate table with many-to-many, (3) a hierarchy with parents. Each hypothesis has a testable consequence (see ADI in a separate breakdown).

02 · Modeling

Edge cases surface

User stories («as a user, I want to search notes by a combination of tags»), data model (the tags, note_tags tables), state machine («a tag with 0 notes - is it deleted automatically or does it linger?»). These are deferred to Open Questions.

03 · Architecting

One option with trade-offs

Option 2 is chosen (a separate table). Trade-off: data migration and +1 JOIN on every search, in exchange for many-to-many and normalisation. The hierarchy is deferred into Non-Goals.

04 · Delivery

A 13-step PRD from the PM agent

The BMAD PM agent (John) generates PRD-074 with all 13 sections. You do a manual pass over the quality checkpoints - Information Density / Measurability / Implementation Leakage. Non-Goals and Open Questions are filled in.

05 · Adversarial Review

A new session finds 6 problems

The «find at least 3» prompt returns 6 findings. After filtering: 2 REAL (the edge case «deleting a tag while a search is running» was missed, the performance AC is vague), 3 FALSE POSITIVE (covered in Section 8), 1 NITPICK. The two real fixes go into the PRD. PRD-074 moves to the active status.

How to read this breakdown

  1. Top to bottom, without skipping sections. BMAD gives the phases, the PRD fixes the contract, Adversarial Review checks the honesty. Each concept builds on the previous one.
  2. Notice the colours on the BMAD diagram. Brainstorming - blue (widening), Modeling - green (structure), Architecting - orange (this is the point of choice, the most important moment), Delivery - grey (artifacts go to the team).
  3. Three sections on the PRD card are highlighted in orange. Non-Goals, Risks, Open Questions - exactly the ones engineers skip on reflex. If they are empty in your PRD, that is a signal.
  4. Compare the two Adversarial Review columns. The left one (same session) - 0 findings, hidden problems stay hidden. The right one (new session) - 5-7 findings, of which 2 are real in the example. The difference is not the model, it is the context.
  5. The «Tags + Saved Searches» example is a template. Put your own feature in its place and walk the same five steps. If you get stuck on one of them, that is where your process is weak.
